5. Your target design — is it conceptually possible, and which alignment mechanism

Yes, it's coherent, and the epoch-0 result says the two ingredients already coexist — stage-1 alignment (cross 0.963) plus waypoint-init topology (cohesion 1.6x Leiden) in one assignment. The failure is not that the goals conflict in principle; it's that your current within-batch force destroys the alignment while optimizing, because each batch's graph pulls the shared prototypes toward its own geometry with no correspondence constraint tying the batches together.

Candidate mechanisms, with the honest why-yes/why-no:

A. ANCHOR TO INIT (L2-SP / distillation: penalize KL(current assign || epoch-0
   assign) on train cells)
   + directly implements "preserve"; label-free; standard fine-tuning practice;
     epoch-0 is MEASURED to be the best state, so anchoring to it is safe
   - preserves everything, including whatever the init got wrong; caps how much
     within-batch correction you can buy

B. MNN-STYLE CROSS-BATCH POSITIVES IN THE COHESION GRAPH (pairs found as mutual
   nearest neighbours in the stage-1 latent, added as edges cohesion may use)
   + the alignment becomes part of the SAME objective as within-batch correction,
     in the same currency (graph pairs); purely data-driven -- a batch-unique type
     simply has no MNN partners, so nothing pushes it to mix (your constraint)
   + section 4's protection mechanism operates THROUGH exactly these pairs
   - the pairs are only as good as stage-1's alignment (it distills stage 1);
     ~58% of cells currently lack cross-batch edges, MNN would have to be dense
     enough; and it reopens the 9d decision to exclude cross-batch pairs (that
     exclusion was argued for ZEROS -- cross-batch POSITIVES were never the problem)

C. FREEZE PROTOTYPES, TRAIN ENCODER ONLY (block route (b) structurally)
   + the only way to raise cohesion becomes moving z smoothly; cheapest change
   - the init's prototype placement becomes a hard ceiling

D. EXPLICIT BATCH-MIXING PRESSURE (entropy rewards etc.)
   - ruled out by your own constraint, and rightly: it falsely mixes batch-unique
     types; the overcorrection literature is the cautionary tale

My reading of the evidence: B is the mechanism that matches your stated goal — biology-driven cross-batch alignment living inside the clustering objective — with A or C as the cheap guardrail while B is built. And the section-3/4 measurements should come first, because they test the premises (CVAE merges rare; alignment protects) before you invest in the mechanism built on them.

Both measurement designs are free. Say which you want first — or whether to write this whole conceptual frame into an ADR before anything else, which is what I'd do, since it's the first time the project has a falsifiable account of what each term can and cannot do.

Your biological story is right, and it's worth making the mechanism mathematically exact — because when you do, it turns out to be two multiplicative suppressions, not one, and it makes your cancer-marker example the literal definition of why generalization matters.

The mechanism, made precise

The recon loss is a double sum — over cells and over genes:

loss = mean over CELLS of ( sum over GENES of (x_g - xhat_g)^2 )

A rare type's marker signature is penalized twice:

suppression 1 (cell frequency)   the type is 1% of cells -> its errors enter the
                                 average with weight 0.01
suppression 2 (gene count)       its signature is ~10 marker genes out of 4,000
                                 -> even for ITS OWN cells, getting the markers
                                 wrong costs ~10/4000 of that cell's loss

total stake of the marker signal in the objective:
   0.01 x (10/4000) x effect^2  ~  a few parts in 100,000

Now the bottleneck: latent_dims = 8, L2-normalized, plus KL pulling toward one prior. The encoder must discard most of the 4,000-dim signal, and what it discards is ranked by exactly this stake — the reconstruction-variance each direction explains. A subtle marker of a rare type is the first thing on the chopping block, not by accident but as the optimum of the objective. So "the encoder omits looking at those genes" is not a failure of training; it is what a well-trained 8-dim reconstruction encoder should do. That's why the fix has to be a different objective, not better optimization.

Why your cancer example is the sharpest version of the generalization problem

This connects directly to the route-(a)/route-(b) distinction from last message. Suppose training does group the rare cells correctly — but via route (b), graph memorization. Then:

train cells with the marker   -> correctly grouped (the graph knew them by name)
a NEW patient's cell with the marker -> placed by the ENCODER ALONE
                              -> the encoder never learned to read the marker gene
                              -> filed with common cells. The marker is invisible.

So transductive success is clinically worthless for your use case: the deployment scenario — examine a new cell — only ever touches the encoder map. The epoch-0 result showed stage 2 improves train-side grouping while making the map worse; in your example's terms, that is optimizing the atlas while degrading the diagnostic. This is the strongest argument you have for why the within-batch correction must be forced through the encoder (route a) and not the prototypes (route b).

One honest refinement: the bottleneck is a cascade, and your fix acts at one stage

The marker signal passes through four compressions, each variance-weighted:

raw genes  ->  HVG 4,000  ->  PCA 50 (the GRAPH lives here)  ->  CVAE latent 8

Your premise — "the graph says the rare cells are similar" — holds only if the marker survived to stage 3. The measured evidence says on these datasets it largely does: SEACells, working at the 50-PC graph level, keeps rare homogeneity 0.911, while Leiden on the 8-dim latent gets 0.736. That gap — graph knows, latent forgot — is precisely the information your cohesion term is supposed to pull back into the partition. But if a marker already died at HVG or PCA, nothing downstream can resurrect it; that's a scope limit to state, and it's checkable per marker.

This is also a real literature anchor for you: the rare-cell-detection line (GiniClust, Jiang et al., Genome Biology 2016; FiRE, Jindal et al.) exists precisely because variance-weighted feature selection and embedding miss rare-population markers — they replace variance ranking with rarity-aware statistics. Verify the exact citations before the paper, but the premise of that literature is your claim.

2. "Boundary carving" — the picture

The assignment is argmax_k dot(z, proto_k), which slices the latent into K regions separated by boundaries. Now suppose train cells i, j are graph-connected but sit on opposite sides of a boundary. Two ways the gradient can fix it:

RULE:   change the encoder so the PATTERN i and j share maps them together
        -> every future cell with that pattern also lands together
CARVE:  nudge z_i by a tiny amount so it hops the boundary, or shift the
        prototype so the boundary passes on the other side of these two points
        -> fixes i and j, means nothing for any new cell

Carving is like gerrymandering districts around known voters. In 8 dimensions, a flexible encoder can move individual points without much affecting the overall map — locally sharp warps. The measured signature: train cohesion +50% while the latent degrades as a neighbour space (0.891 → 0.864).
