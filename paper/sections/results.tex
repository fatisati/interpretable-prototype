\section{Experiments}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We evaluate on three multi-batch single-cell RNA-seq datasets~\citep{luecken2020benchmarking}:
\textbf{Pancreas} (16,382 cells, 9 technologies, 14 cell types),
\textbf{PBMC (immune)} (30k cells, 5 batches, 9 cell types),
and a \textbf{Lung} dataset (30k cells, 16 batches, 17 cell types).
For spatial evaluations, we use a \textbf{non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)} spatial transcriptomics dataset~\citep{pentimalli2025combining}.
The original dataset comprises 6 slides; we select slide 28, subsample 30,000 cells stratified by cell type and niche label, and exclude cells assigned to uninformative niche labels, yielding 28,804 cells across 18 cell types and 9 niches.
Niche labels are defined by clustering cells based on the local cell-type composition of their spatial neighborhood, providing a measure of microenvironmental context that is related to but distinct from the spatial affinity used during training.
All datasets are preprocessed with Scanpy~\citep{wolf2018scanpy} by normalizing total counts, applying log1p transformation, and selecting 4,000 highly variable genes.

\paragraph{Affinity construction.}
For transcriptomic experiments, cell--cell affinities are computed using an adaptive Gaussian kernel on PCA embeddings, following prior work~\citep{seacells}.
For spatial experiments, we apply the same kernel to spatially aggregated features, obtained by averaging the PCA embeddings of neighboring cells within a fixed spatial radius, thereby incorporating microenvironmental context into the affinity (see Appendix~\ref{app:spatial_affinity} for details).

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against \textbf{SEACells}~\citep{seacells}, a metacell method based on archetypal analysis;
\textbf{MetaQ}~\citep{metaq}, which learns discrete metacell representations end-to-end via vector quantization but lacks explicit batch correction;
\textbf{scPoli (cVAE)}, scPoli's cVAE trained without its supervised prototype loss, providing a reconstruction-only baseline that isolates the contribution of scProto's additional objectives;
and \textbf{Parametric UMAP}, which preserves data geometry by aligning pairwise relationships in the embedding space.
Since scPoli (cVAE) and Parametric UMAP do not produce discrete cluster assignments, we apply $K$-means clustering on their learned embedding spaces to obtain metacells, using the same $K$ as scProto.
For spatial experiments, we additionally evaluate \textbf{SEACells (spatial)}, which replaces SEACells' default PCA-based affinity graph with the spatial context affinity while keeping the archetypal analysis step unchanged.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
scProto outputs soft prototype assignments $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^K$; each cell is assigned to the prototype with the highest score (argmax), and each metacell is labeled by majority voting.
We assess community structure using \textbf{modularity}, cross-batch integration using \textbf{batch entropy}, biological signal preservation using \textbf{cell-type purity}, rare cell representation using \textbf{coverage} and \textbf{rare purity}, and for spatial data, microenvironmental structure using \textbf{niche purity}.
Formal definitions are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}.

\subsection{scProto recovers community structure while integrating cells across batches}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:graph_batch}, scProto achieves modularity comparable to SEACells, indicating that it recovers the community structure of the input affinity graph. At the same time, it exhibits substantially higher batch entropy, demonstrating effective mixing of cells across batches. Importantly, this is achieved without compromising biological identity, as scProto maintains cell-type purity comparable to competing methods.

In contrast, SEACells attains similar modularity and purity but exhibits very low batch entropy, indicating that it forms batch-specific metacells without meaningful cross-batch aggregation. Conversely, scPoli (cVAE) and Parametric UMAP achieve high batch entropy and purity but show significantly lower modularity, suggesting that they fail to recover the community structure encoded in the affinity graph.

These differences are further illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:pancreas_umap}, where UMAP~\citep{mcinnes2018umap} visualizations of pancreas metacells show that scProto forms clusters that are well aligned across batches while remaining consistent with cell-type structure, whereas SEACells produces clusters that are largely separated by batch.
Per-metacell distributions of purity and batch entropy are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:purity_entropy} (Appendix).

\subsection{Cross-Batch Metacells Improve Representation of Rare Cell Types}

We next examine whether cross-batch aggregation improves the representation of low-frequency (rare) cell types.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:rare_cells}, scProto achieves consistently higher or comparable coverage across datasets, indicating that aggregating cells across batches enables the formation of metacells for cell types that are underrepresented within individual batches. Importantly, scProto also attains higher or comparable purity for metacells corresponding to rare cell types, demonstrating that it groups cells more coherently despite limited per-batch samples.

To further analyze performance across the frequency spectrum, we stratify cell types by frequency and evaluate metacell purity within each bin (Fig.~\ref{fig:rare_purity}, Appendix). scProto maintains high purity even for the rarest cell types, whereas SEACells shows a noticeable degradation in low-frequency bins. This suggests that restricting aggregation within batches limits the ability to form reliable metacells when only few samples are available per batch.

In contrast, reconstruction-based methods such as scPoli (cVAE), and structure-preserving methods such as Parametric UMAP, do not explicitly enforce community-level structure during training. As a result, they tend to merge rare cell states into larger, denser clusters, leading to reduced purity for low-frequency populations.

\subsection{scProto captures microenvironmental structure while preserving cell-type identity}

We evaluate whether defining cell--cell affinity from spatial context enables scProto to capture microenvironmental structure without sacrificing cell-type identity.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_tradeoff} for four cell types spanning the malignant, stromal, and immune compartments of the NSCLC tumor microenvironment, scProto achieves higher niche purity than transcriptomics-only methods---SEACells and MetaQ---which fail to capture spatial organization, while maintaining substantially higher cell-type purity than SEACells trained on spatial affinity, which improves niche purity at the cost of biological specificity.
This balance arises because scProto's reconstruction objective anchors prototypes to gene expression even when guided by spatial affinity: the model identifies expression patterns that correlate with the local microenvironment without losing cell-type identity.
In contrast, transcriptomics-only methods receive no spatial signal and cannot recover niche structure, while SEACells trained on spatial affinity lacks a reconstruction constraint, allowing spatial proximity to dominate over transcriptional identity.

These results show that scProto captures niche-correlated transcriptional programs---expression variation associated with the local cellular microenvironment---while remaining faithful to cell-type identity.
This is distinct from modeling direct cell-cell communication; rather, defining affinity spatially guides scProto to discover gene expression structure that reflects microenvironmental context, a signal that transcriptomic similarity alone cannot recover.
Spatial visualizations for the same three cell types in Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial} (Appendix) further confirm that scProto groups cells from the same niches while maintaining coherent cell-type structure. A per-cell-type breakdown for additional cell types is provided in Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_heatmap} (Appendix).
